Inbox Cleaner

Gmail makes you delete email one message at a time. Nobody has 40,000 email problems — they have 200 sender problems. This inverts the unit of work, and then spends the whole design budget on making bulk destruction feel safe.

The Challenge

The Challenge

Gmail's interface is built around the message and the thread. Search-select-all-delete technically exists, but it's hostile: you can't see what you're about to destroy, the selection silently caps at the visible page, and there's no undo worth the name. So people don't use it, and the inbox grows forever. Inverting to senders makes the decisions tractable — but it also means every action now operates on thousands of irreversible items at once, against an API that rate-limits hard and can fail halfway through.

Strategic Directive

Strategic Objective

Make the sender the unit of work, then earn the right to act at that scale — by making every action reversible, every partial failure legible, and the blast radius of any single click visible before it's taken. The ceiling on damage should be structural, not procedural.

Process
PHASE 01

Indexing 32,000 Messages Inside Quota

  • Fetching full messages is impossible within quota; fetching header metadata is not — so the index pulls only From, Subject, Date, and List-Unsubscribe, batched 50 per request
  • Gmail allows 250 quota units per second, which puts a large inbox at roughly 13 minutes of continuous fetching — far too long for a blocking spinner
  • Senders appear and their counts climb as pages land, newest-first, so the table is usable at 500 messages and complete at 32,000. Throttling is adaptive and says so: "Gmail is throttling — still working" rather than appearing frozen

PHASE 02

Choosing Metrics That Earn Their Column

  • Read rate turned out to be the single best signal of whether a sender matters — a high count paired with 2% read is an instant, confident decision
  • The List-Unsubscribe header is free in metadata, so it became a bulk action of its own — stopping the flow at the source instead of only clearing the backlog, which is the difference between cleaning an inbox once and keeping it clean
  • It's the only action offered on senders that don't support it — the button disables rather than hides, so the reason stays visible instead of the control silently vanishing
  • Sort defaults to message count descending, because the biggest wins should require no interaction at all to find

PHASE 03

Making Bulk Action Survivable

  • The app requests modify permission but never delete permission — it can move mail to Trash, where it's recoverable for 30 days, and cannot permanently destroy anything. A ceiling on damage that a bug can't exceed
  • Before every batch, an operation record captures the prior label state of every message id, so undo is an exact inverse rather than a guess. A 60-second countdown toast holds it, and the last 20 operations stay individually undoable
  • Friction scales with blast radius: a preview panel always, the count as the button's own label past 500 messages, and type-to-confirm past 5,000 or for anything going to Trash — but never for unsubscribe or mark-read, where nothing is removed and a typed confirmation would be theatre

Reflection

The most useful constraint came from outside the product. Reading Gmail metadata requires restricted scopes, which means an annual third-party security assessment before an app can be published publicly — thousands of dollars a year, forever, for a free tool. Rather than water down the product, it pushed the architecture somewhere better: no server at all, browser-side auth, and each user bringing their own OAuth client so they sit inside Google's personal-use exemption. The privacy claim stopped being a promise and became structural — there's nothing for me to leak because I never hold anything. What I'd do differently: I designed the happy path first and retrofitted partial-failure handling, when "chunk 3 of 7 failed and 3,000 messages moved" is the state that actually decides whether someone trusts the tool again.
