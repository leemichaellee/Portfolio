Split

A dinner bill splitter built around the parts every other splitter gets wrong: shared items, proportional tax and tip, and the leftover penny that stops the numbers from reconciling.

Assign, reconcile, settle. The running total is visible at every step, and it always equals the bill.

Project Overview & Context
Role Everything — product logic, interaction design, front end
Timeframe 2026 · Self-directed
Driver A real recurring annoyance with a group of ~60 dancers

The Challenge

The Challenge

Every bill splitter nails the easy case and fumbles three hard ones. Splitting tip evenly taxes the person who ordered a salad to subsidize the person who ordered a steak. Shared items — the wine, the appetizers — are treated as an edge case when they're the common case. And $100 split three ways is $33.33 three times, which is $99.99: somebody has to absorb that cent, and if nobody does, the totals stop reconciling. Once your app hands a table five numbers that don't add up to the bill, nobody at that table opens it again.

Strategic Directive

Strategic Objective

Make the arithmetic provably correct and then make it invisible. The sum of what everyone owes must equal the bill total exactly, every time, asserted in code rather than hoped for — and the interaction on top of it has to be fast enough to finish before the server comes back for the card.

Process
PHASE 01

Integer Cents and the Largest-Remainder Rule

  • All money is stored as integer cents — no floating point anywhere in the calculation path, which removes an entire category of drift
  • Proportional allocation uses the largest-remainder method: floor every share, then hand the leftover cents to the largest fractional remainders
  • Tie-breaking is seeded from the bill id, so reopening or resharing the same bill always produces the same assignment — not random, not always the first person

PHASE 02

Refusing to Absorb the Difference

  • Receipts carry things the line items don't — service charges, corkage, a discount, an OCR miss — so the item sum rarely matches the printed subtotal exactly
  • Most apps quietly swallow that gap. This one surfaces it: "$2.18 unaccounted for", with three ways to resolve it and no way to proceed by ignoring it
  • The same reconciliation step is the backstop that catches bad OCR, which is why the parsed receipt is always shown as an editable draft rather than committed straight to the split

PHASE 03

Disclosing the Penny

  • Whoever absorbs an extra cent gets a small +1¢ marker on their row — it costs nothing to disclose and it's what makes the rest of the math believable
  • Only bill-level rounding is marked. Per-item remainders wash out across a dozen items and would badge everybody, which communicates nothing
  • A persistent footer asserts the invariant in plain language: everyone's totals add to exactly the bill total, no cents lost

Outcomes
0¢ Rounding Drift Per-person totals sum to the bill total exactly on every recalculation. The invariant is asserted after each change; if it ever failed, the app would show a reconciliation banner instead of wrong numbers.
1 tap To Split an Item An "Everyone" control on each row handles appetizers and drinks — the majority of shared items — without opening a share sheet.
No server Architecture OCR runs in the browser and the full bill state encodes into the share link, so there's no account, no backend, and nothing to host beyond static files.

Reflection

The interesting design work turned out to be in the disclosures, not the flow. Showing the +1¢ marker, naming the unaccounted delta, and putting "tipping on the tax adds $3.06" inline next to the toggle — none of those make the app easier to use, and all of them make it easier to believe, which is the thing that actually decides whether a group uses it twice. What I'd change: I designed assignment before reconciliation, and reconciliation is the harder problem. Starting from "the receipt is wrong and the items are wrong" would have produced a better editing model than the one I retrofitted.
