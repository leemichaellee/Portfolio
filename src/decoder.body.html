Plain-English Decoder

A reader for documents written to protect someone else — leases, terms of service, loan disclosures. The design problem wasn't summarizing them. It was making a summary you'd be willing to act on.

Every plain sentence on the right is anchored to the exact clause on the left. Selecting either side highlights the other.

The Challenge

The Challenge

Asking a language model to explain a lease is a five-minute build. The problem is what happens next: the model smooths a hostile clause into something that sounds reasonable, the reader relaxes, and the reader is wrong. In this category a confident summary is worse than no summary, because it replaces a document the person would have read carefully with a paraphrase they trust completely. Every design decision had to answer the same question — why should someone believe this?

Strategic Directive

Strategic Objective

Build a reader where the plain-English output can't outrun its evidence. Anchor every sentence to the clause it came from, make verification a one-second glance rather than a re-read, and design an explicit state for the clauses the model can't handle — so that the system's limits are visible in the interface instead of hidden inside confident prose.

Process
PHASE 01

Making the Output Structural, Not Prose

  • Split the document into clauses — the smallest unit carrying a complete obligation, cost, or condition — rather than paragraphs or sentences
  • Each clause returns a typed object: plain text, category, severity, a confidence score, and the exact character range it covers
  • Because the output is structured, the interface can reason about it — sort by severity, roll up risk flags, and refuse to render a card whose span doesn't validate

PHASE 02

Designing the Verification View

  • Two panes, permanently bound — hovering a card highlights its source, clicking scrolls the original to it, selecting original text scrolls to the card that owns it
  • There is deliberately no view that shows the plain English alone, because alone it is just a claim
  • The four fixed summary questions — what this costs you, what you must do, what you give up, how you get out — are assembled from clause data, never from a second pass over the document, so the summary can't assert something no clause supports

PHASE 03

Designing the Admission of Doubt

  • Below 60% confidence the card stops answering: it shows the original text and says plainly that it isn't confident enough to restate it
  • Clauses that are already clear get a third state — the original, tagged "already clear" — instead of being paraphrased into different clear text
  • Treating "I don't know" as a designed success state rather than an error is what makes the confident cards worth believing

Outcomes

Reflection

The thing I'd carry into any AI feature is that confidence is an interface problem before it's a model problem. The version of this that only showed plain English tested worse with me than the two-pane version, even though the text was identical — because I couldn't check it, so I didn't trust it, so I re-read the lease anyway and the tool had saved me nothing. What I'd do differently: I built the unresolved state last, and it should have been first. Designing the failure case early would have forced the structured output format sooner instead of my retrofitting spans onto prose I'd already shipped.
