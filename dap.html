DAP 2.2 — Accessibility & Rationalization

Owning the asset allocation portion of a $4.7M, legally-driven accessibility remediation and rationalization program spanning 34 legacy properties — where the hard problem wasn't designing from scratch, but renegotiating an entrenched visual pattern under a compliance deadline.

Role
Led asset allocation workstream, working alongside an XD Principal as a collaborator
Status
All 34 URLs remediated; staged public release in progress
Scale
$4.7M program · 34 URLs/properties
Driver
ADA / WCAG legal and regulatory exposure
Timeframe
Program started Q1 2024 · I joined Q3 2024 · rolled off Q2 2026 · staged release continues into 2026

Problem framing

This program existed because of legal exposure, not as a discretionary redesign — ADA/WCAG compliance risk was the forcing function across the whole initiative.

My portion focused on asset allocation: a full page within the Merrill digital experience that gives clients a breakdown of their portfolio construction — used to assess risk and diversification, validate balance and composition, and do an overall portfolio health check. The original experience carried a high cognitive load: dense, unprioritized data with no clear visual or information hierarchy, split across two tabs — a pie chart for a high-level overview, and a separate data table that dove into each individual security or holding. The chart's specific accessibility failure was color contrast.

The core tension: remediating for accessibility meant changing a visual pattern that had been in production and in the brand system for years. This wasn't a blank-canvas design problem — it was renegotiating an existing, entrenched pattern under a legal deadline, in a way that stayed usable and on-brand.

Constraints

Accessibility vs. existing brand/visual patterns

We pushed for new visual design colors for the allocation chart, which then became part of the shared design system — now used to denote specific asset classes across Merrill (cash = green, equities = dark blue, fixed income = purple, alternative investments = orange). There was no real pushback from brand or visual stakeholders on this change.

Scale and consistency across legacy tech stacks

The properties in scope ran on different platforms and templates, so a pattern that worked on one didn't necessarily ship the same way everywhere. Part of the job was defining a standard that could actually be implemented consistently despite that.

Budget and scope pressure

As a high-visibility, high-cost program, decisions were scrutinized closely, which shaped where we could pursue the ideal fix versus a phased or scoped-down one.

Regulatory deadline

WCAG conformance isn't negotiable on timeline the way typical feature work is — that urgency changed how decisions got made: faster, more top-down, less room for iteration than a standard design process would allow.

Decisions

Decision 1 — Restructuring around an "inverse pyramid"

The question
The original page split allocation data across two separate tabs — a chart and a data table — with no clear hierarchy and a high cognitive load. How should the page be restructured to fix that, accessibly?
Options considered
Keep the two-tab structure and remediate each in place, vs. consolidate into a single page with a deliberate information hierarchy.
The call
Landed on an "inverse pyramid" structure: high-level overview information at the top, with the ability to drill down into individual data points (security quantity, price, value, and so on) further down. The two tabs were consolidated into one page — chart at the top, data table underneath.
Why
It replaces implicit tab-switching with a natural top-to-bottom scan, giving users the high-level picture first and depth on demand — which reduces cognitive load and establishes the visual/information hierarchy the original page lacked.
How I led it
I designed this restructuring directly.

Decision 2 — Atomizing the design system for data-dense pages

The question
Keeping a unified feel across 34 URLs running on inconsistent legacy tech stacks, without going off-brand or building a parallel design system from scratch, was a real challenge. How do you standardize something you can't implement identically everywhere?
Options considered
Apply the existing HELIX design system's components as-is (risk: not flexible enough for how data-dense these pages needed to be) vs. break HELIX down to its atomic level and build a local design language on top of it.
The call
Broke HELIX down to atoms — its smallest variations — and used that to create a local design language for account-details pages specifically (asset allocation, holdings, activity, and position details).
Why
This kept a unified feel across related pages without going off-brand, while staying flexible enough to handle screens far more data-dense than most HELIX components were originally built for.
How I led it
I played a pivotal role designing components with enhanced functionality beyond their original HELIX counterparts — including expandable cards with header areas that scale to data-dense headers, and "billboards" used across these account-details pages to surface high-level overview values.

Leadership & collaboration

I worked alongside an XD Principal on the account-details work, as a collaborator — not as their report, and not a title I held myself on this project — owning the asset allocation portion specifically.

This is something I repeat to other teams at the bank when the subject comes up. Because I work on the consumer investing side, I regularly deal with digital experiences that are extremely data-dense — the pattern I push for is to keep stretching the boundaries of the existing design system so it scales to hold up to 5x the data most components were originally built for. In practice that means adjusting spacing, type hierarchy, and grid layout, then feeding those adaptations back into the shared system so other teams facing the same density problem can use them too.

Success measures

Since this program hasn't launched, success is defined through pre-set conformance targets plus audit and usability evidence, rather than shipped metrics.

The target is WCAG 2.2 Level AA conformance, audited by our dedicated ADA partners, who we worked closely with throughout the process. All 34 URLs have been remediated as of now. Release is staged rather than pushed live all at once, so the team can closely monitor how clients react to what are, in places, major UI and UX changes before expanding further.

Reflection

The biggest open risk is adoption: even though functionality stayed the same, these are major visual changes, and it will take clients time to adjust. I'm confident the changes improve the experience and help modernize the platform against other financial institutions — but that's still playing out as the staged release continues.

In hindsight, I'd have thought about component scalability from day one rather than adapting to it as the program grew — given the scale of this project, designing Figma components built to scale from the start would have saved real rework later. That ties to the same broader lesson as the rest of this work: a deeper upfront understanding of how clients actually use the asset allocation page, and the full range of screen variations, would have made the early design decisions stronger.
