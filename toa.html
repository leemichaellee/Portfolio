Unlaunched Design Leadership

Transfer of Assets

Leading design on the flow that handles roughly 100,000 incoming and outgoing digital asset transfers annually — one of the highest-anxiety actions a person takes with their money — across legacy systems, cross-org stakeholders, and real ambiguity about what data the team would ultimately have access to.

Role
Sr. Product Designer, directing 1 designer + 1 content strategist, alongside an XD Principal
Status
In development, unlaunched
Scale
~100,000 digital transfers annually
Timeframe
Started Q2 2025 · MVP May 2027 · Full release Nov 2027–Feb 2028

Problem framing

A transfer of assets moves a person's brokerage or retirement holdings from one institution to another. It's functionally simple to describe and extraordinarily high-stakes to experience — users are moving their life savings, often without a clear sense of what's happening to their money mid-transfer.

For the business, transfers are a major driver of support cost and friction, and a competitive lever: institutions win and lose assets based on how trustworthy and frictionless this moment feels.

This was a design leadership problem rather than a UX problem specifically because the flow crossed systems, teams, and compliance boundaries that no single stakeholder owned end-to-end. Someone had to hold the whole picture and make the flow feel coherent despite that fragmentation.

The project was driven by a combination of competitive pressure and an internal compliance requirement — bringing the flow in line with an updated design system. Today's digital transfer of assets flow still runs on the legacy design system: it's disjointed and heavily dependent on manual, middle-office intervention. The broader shift is toward open finance — using API data scrapes to prefill personal, account, and asset information — to automate as much of the process as possible.

Constraints

Legacy systems and technical debt

The core issue wasn't a single inflexible system integration so much as the absence of a defined design system in the legacy flow. There was no consistent pattern language to build from — part of the work was establishing new interaction and visual standards as part of the redesign itself, not just applying an existing one.

Cross-org stakeholder complexity

Stakeholders included product partners and development teams. Interests didn't conflict heavily, but real constraints existed around funding and around which device/platform to prioritize for MVP. The team decided MVP would be mobile-first: mobile could be iframed into the existing app for a more seamless experience, and a mobile-first design covers the most ground, since it extends to the desktop experience as well.

User trust and anxiety

Moving a life savings is not a neutral transaction. Tone, transparency, and error handling all had to be designed around the emotional stakes of the action, not just its mechanics.

Ambiguity in data access

At the time of design, it wasn't fully known what data the team would ultimately be able to surface — transfer status, custodian information, and similar details were still being negotiated with upstream systems. The flow had to stay coherent and trustworthy across multiple possible data-availability scenarios, not just the ideal one.

This constraint played out concretely through what the team calls the "Bank Link" screens — already-designed screens whose UI can't be changed beyond minor copy edits. For MVP, our API provider connects to the four most common external financial institutions to pull data from (opening up to more counterparties at full release). Whatever data comes back gets prefilled automatically; the client only has to fill in the gaps. Design-wise, that meant building something that leverages the design system's input field types to scale to any number of missing fields, since the exact gaps vary depending on what the API was able to return for a given institution.

Decisions

Decision 1 — Moving document upload before submission

The question
In the legacy (BAU) experience, clients upload required documentation — a signed transfer form, identity verification, and similar — after they submit the transfer request. When that documentation is missing or wrong, the transfer becomes "Not In Good Order" (NIGO): it doesn't go through, and it sits in a pile, generating friction for the client and manual follow-up work for middle office. Should the new flow keep that ordering?
Options considered
Keep the BAU pattern (collect documents after submission, chase missing ones after the fact) vs. move document upload earlier, before the transfer request can be submitted at all.
The call
Require document upload prior to transfer request submission.
Why
A transfer can't enter a stalled, NIGO state if it can't be submitted without its required documentation in the first place — this removes the failure mode at its source rather than handling it after the fact.
How I led it
I pushed for this reordering as a strategic move, against the grain of the existing BAU pattern.

Decision 2 — Scoping the form to only what's missing

The question
When the API returns incomplete data for a given institution — a missing full account number, account type, or joint account holder names — how much should the client be asked to fill in?
Options considered
Show a full, standard form regardless of what data came back, vs. dynamically scope the form to only the specific fields that are actually missing, prefilling everything else from data already on file.
The call
Only surface input fields for the missing data points, prefilled with whatever is already known from existing records.
Why
It reduces cognitive load on the client — they're never re-entering information the institution already has, and the amount of manual entry scales with how much the API was actually able to return.
How I led it
I defined this as the governing pattern for the form, building it to scale to any number of missing fields using the design system's input field types.

Leadership & team

I directed a team of one designer and one content strategist, and operated as the primary design voice across a cross-functional org that included compliance, operations, and engineering — without formal authority over those disciplines. I worked closely with an XD Principal on the effort, who operated in a project-management-like capacity; design direction was mine to set.

One unblock reached beyond this project to other designers: how error states get handed off to development. The flow is form-heavy, and many fields can carry anywhere from one to five different error scenarios — mocking every permutation for every screen was slow and didn't scale. I created a new guideline: build one error-state variation per screen to show the UI pattern, then use Figma annotations to spell out the specific error copy needed by dev and QA for each scenario. At least one other project has since adopted it.

On direct mentorship: our designer needed support building a deeper understanding of the project's constraints and the complexity of the flows involved. Part of the job was untangling that complexity for them so they could design more effectively — rather than leaving them to work it out unassisted against a deadline.

Success measures

Because this flow hasn't launched yet, success here is defined rather than measured — the targets the team committed to before launch, and how we planned to validate them.

Two targets anchor this: a 30% reduction in NIGOs — driven directly by moving document collection ahead of submission — and a 15% increase in transfer completion rate, driven by the combination of NIGO reduction and the reduced friction of a form that only asks for what's actually missing.

Reflection

The plan is solid, and most screens are already designed. The real risks heading into MVP are making sure every screen lands fully Helix and ADA compliant, and how much data the API integration actually ends up being able to return — the designs account for both, but how much the experience genuinely improves depends on how little manual entry clients end up needing, which isn't fully in our control yet.

If I were doing this again, I'd spend more time on strategic design direction earlier and less time eager to get into the weeds of designing every individual screen. That's a deliberate shift as I push toward more lead-level work: a greater emphasis on design direction, grounded in a deeper understanding of the product and the experience, before diving into screens.
